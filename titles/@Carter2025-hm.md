> [!abstract]
> Hugo Münsterberg was born 1863 in Danzig. His early education was diverse, and included writing poetry, and studying art, science, philosophy, and music. At 19, he began medical studies at the University of Leipzig, where he encountered Wilhelm Wundt and became inspired to pursue psychology. Münsterberg’s Ph.D. dissertation, which focused on individual differences, was controversial and diverged from Wundt’s views. After completing his Ph.D. in 1885, Münsterberg worked at Freiburg University, where he conducted research on perception and published significant work, including a rejected thesis idea from Wundt. In 1892, William James invited him to Harvard, where Münsterberg quickly adapted to speaking English and became a prominent lecturer and researcher. Münsterberg had a strong influence on the development of multiple fields including forensic psychology, film studies, psychotherapy, and of course industrial-organizational (I-O) psychology. His research on employee selection and his advocating for a focus on worker well-being was ahead of its time. He died in 1916 while delivering a lecture. His work received mixed reviews from subsequent psychologists citing his subjective approach, however, it is difficult to disentangle this later criticism from national politics, which caused strained relations between himself and American colleagues as he defended Germany as World War I began. Regardless, Münsterberg’s legacy includes mentoring future leaders in psychology, advocating for women in the field, and his efforts to bridge American–German cultural understanding. His impact on applied psychology and his role in shaping I-O psychology were undeniably significant.
