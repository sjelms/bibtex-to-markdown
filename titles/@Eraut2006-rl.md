> [!abstract]
> The concept of ‘method’ plays an important role in all three domains that concern the readers of this journal: professional practice; education; and research. The Oxford English Dictionary suggests two core meanings of ‘method’. The first is a procedure or way of doing things, and the second a systematic or orderly arrangement of actions or ideas. The two are often linked together, because professionals use methods to provide short, common descriptions of what they do and how they do it. Attaching names to procedures, or clusters of procedures, gives an impression of uniformity or regularity, even when they may not be performed consistently by all practitioners on all occasions. When we make strategic decisions in teaching and research, we first seek to clarify our purpose, then we decide what methods we want to use. More detailed objectives and more detailed specifications of those methods follow later and tend to be more client and context specific. Although many of the components in a care plan might be described as methods, the term ‘method’ is used less in health and social care, perhaps because there are so many possible components. When practitioners in health and social care state that they are using a particular method, this normally implies that they, or someone else, has made a choice of method(s) and is prepared to justify that choice. But, if they said that they were following a particular practice, one could not assume that there had been a conscious choice. Practices are more likely to be taken for granted as ‘the way we do things here’, and they also tend to be situated more in the detail of clients and context. Although the terms ‘method’ and ‘practice’ are sometimes used almost synonymously, I think it is useful to think of method as ‘what people decide to do’ and practice as ‘what people do’, and the deciders are not always the same as the doers. Methods are important because they are not just ways of organizing our actions, they are also ways of conceptualizing what we think we are doing. Returning to the Oxford English Dictionary's second core meaning of ‘method’ as a systematic or orderly arrangement of ideas and actions, suggests that the term also covers classification systems or typologies, sequences of decisions (e.g. algorithms) or procedures (e.g. care pathways), and commonly used combinations of methods. All of these play a major role in the conceptualization of practice. Sometimes, however, what gets called a ‘method’ is determined more by professional tradition than by any discernible logic. In higher education teaching, the term ‘method’ is used with a very broad brush. When Higher Education teachers fill in a course proposal form, they describe their teaching methods in terms of lectures, seminars, tutorials, practicals and workshops, and books on HE teaching use similar categories. However, these broad descriptions often tell us more about the rooms in which teaching occurs than about how the teaching is done. We know that the term ‘lecture’ usually implies a low level of interaction between the teacher and students, but this may be equally true of many seminars and even tutorials. Indeed, a tutorial, in one context, might have as many students as a seminar in another context. Hence, it is wise to think of a teaching method as a cluster of practices, and to recognize that some of these practices may be closer to practices categorized under another label than they are to practices bearing the same label. Seminars, in particular, might have a within-method variation in student participation between faculties that is as large as that of the between-methods variation in other faculties. This suggests that the standard typology of teaching methods may not address the most significant variables. For example, the often debated issues of teaching being either teacher centred or student centred, and highly structured or loosely structured, do not distinguish in practice between different teaching methods. A similar problem arises with research methods. These are as broadly conceived as teaching methods, and probably have an even greater within-method variation. The two most frequently used methods – questionnaires and interviews – take a very wide range of forms and can be combined in several different ways. However, they cut right across the methodological debate about qualitative and quantitative methods. Only multiple response answers and rating scales leave no scope for qualitative analysis, and no text or transcript is immune from quantitative content analysis. The current obsession with paradigm wars distracts attention from the many creative ways in which methods and combinations of methods can be used and their data analysed and interpreted. In this context, the usage of the term ‘method’ must surely be redefined. There is much conceptual work to be done in teaching and learning and related research. Some classification is needed of different uses of a method according to those features that are believed best to differentiate between them. It might be helpful to have more than one such classification, but not too many, and they need to be well theorized and revised in the light of research. It should take us forward from treating methods as single entities. What is the point of comparative studies of new ‘methods’, such as problem-based learning or enquiry-based learning, if they do not offer considerable detail about the particular form of the methods, the context and the students being compared? Even when the context and the students are the same for both groups, they are still important for judging the possible generalization of the results. Researchers need to ask not whether method A is better than method B, but under what conditions is method A better or worse than method B. I am not suggesting that some conceptual work has not been done, only that some researchers and most university teachers have not given much attention to it, and that this prevents us from gaining the understanding and evidence we need for improving our practices. The other area in need of attention is the semantics of methods. Methods and their associated features often carry a variety of names, and these names often have overlapping, but not identical, meanings. Some of the differences are easily distinguished, because they indicate clearly defined differences in practice. Others just carry different connotations, either by implicitly emphasizing one feature more than another, or by their association with different beliefs and values. Hence, an important attribute of a method is its currency, namely the extent to which the method is shared among the relevant communities of practitioners, educators and researchers. Do they use the same terminology, and does it carry the same meaning across the whole community? The use of typologies for assisting conceptualization is nicely exemplified by Buchler's (1961) analysis of method itself, which is presented in Table 1 below. He argued that according to the model of practice considered desirable, the process of professional education would need to take a very different form. The left column leads to an emphasis on methodic training, careful analysis and planned activity, and the right column leads to an emphasis on a variety of experience, responsiveness, invention and a quick reading of a situation as it develops. One is anticipatory and emphasizes a store of techniques prepared in advance and teaching which to use when. The second is responsive and emphasizes resourceful practice in the face of the unanticipated. Not surprisingly, the balance of emphasis during professional formation is frequently in dispute. Although there are many aspects of Buchler's model that I question, its great merit is the number of questions it raises. For example, my own interest in the link between modes of cognition and speed of action suggests that a more helpful set of headings would be routines, well-defined problems and ill-defined problems. Most professional practice in health and social care involves a combination of regular routine procedures from the left column with decision-making situations that correspond more to the central or right columns. Another debatable feature of the typology is the use of the terms ‘familiarity’ and ‘novel’ for outcomes, but not for the situations encountered. If we consider the situations or client conditions encountered, then we have to consider the experience of the practitioners. The wider their experience, the greater will be the proportion of familiar situations; and the greater the repertoire of the practitioners, the easier it will be for them to anticipate the needs of clients who significantly differ from common types of cases. Similarly, less-experienced practitioners may have learned to handle common cases in a fairly routine way, but still take some time to solve well-defined problems they have not previously experienced; while more experienced practitioners may solve well-defined problems more quickly, but still take considerable time over deciding how to approach most ill-defined problems. Another important change that I would like to make concerns Buchler's use of the word ‘idiosyncratic’, particularly in the central column. My argument is that in many types of work, standard methods have to be adapted for different clients, and that often there are recognized adaptations for different types of client which are widely recognized and far from being idiosyncratic. It is even questionable whether variations within these adaptations should be described as idiosyncratic. It depends on whether this finer variation is triggered by differences in the client, for which the practitioner has a flexible response, or by differences in the way the practitioner likes to do things. In the latter case, it is correct to call the practice idiosyncratic, but such variations might also have the potential for discovering more or less effective versions of the accepted practice. The problem is that, in the absence of research which is sensitive to such variations, the potential good or harm of idiosyncratic variation is unlikely to be noticed. Once more, it is possible to transform the way we conceptualize these variations by changing the terminology. Just as I prefer to use Elstein, Schulman & Sprafka's (1978) distinction between well-defined and ill-defined problems, I prefer to describe standard adaptations of a method as ‘coarse tuning’ and variations within those adaptations as ‘fine tuning’. These terms assume that the practitioner is responding to variations in the client, rather than being idiosyncratic; and if someone was concerned about the risk, they might do best to treat it as a client-centred variation that still needs to be evaluated. An important feature of the tuning metaphor is its greater concern for meeting the needs of each individual client as closely as possible. It also raises the possibility of a more interactive relationship between the diagnosis or assessment of a client and the consequent decisions and/or care plans. There is a danger of perceiving the choice of a clinical method or care plan as a single ‘high stakes’ decision, whereas in many forms of practice a series of small decisions is more probable (Orasanu & Connolly 1993). The practitioner starts with a small intervention, then assesses its effect before making the next decision, etc. Changes in a client's condition over time are always important. Regardless of whether they can be attributed to a particular intervention, they still provide useful further evidence for the ongoing decision-making process. The tuning metaphor also works well for teaching in both education and practice settings. In education settings, there is a clear sequence of both planned and spontaneous decision-making. Decisions about the teaching methods are made when the course is designed and approved. Adaptations, usually familiar to the teachers, if not to their colleagues, are made when preparing for a specific teaching session, but may have to be modified during that session. A great deal of fine-tuning is made, often spontaneously, while teaching. When teaching in clinical settings, however, a more flexible approach is needed. Coaching sessions or consultations may be planned in advance, then postponed, or even cancelled, when unanticipated service needs arise. Much teaching on-the-job may be opportunistic, taking advantage of learning opportunities as and when they occur. Lack of coaching time often means that, unlike the often-cited coaching models used in sport and the arts, learners in health in social care obtain little more than an initial introduction to an important skill. Recognition of the need for further tuning is often conspicuously absent. Similar phenomena are encountered in research. The design of a questionnaire is also a planned focusing process, which at its best involves interaction with potential respondents at every stage: the choice and formatting of the questions and the precise wording. Initial consultation will help with the first draft. Piloting the first draft will often be coarse tuning, especially if you consult with respondents about fitness for purpose as well as the wording; and later piloting will be fine-tuning. There is an element in preparing for interviews that resembles this, but most interviews are conducted with an emphasis on flexible response to what your respondent is saying. However, a very flexible process does encounter certain problems. The most obvious is that if time is limited, important questions may be missed. Less obvious to inexperienced interviewers is the extent to which responses to questions are influenced by the preceding questions that set the context (Eraut 2005). Changing the preceding questions will often change the response. This can help the interviewer who plans a sequence that makes a difficult question seem natural rather than threatening, but can easily lead to questions not being interpreted as intended. The issue in research and also in education and practice is not so much that such problems are not defined, but that they are not even recognized. Hence, interviews need careful preparation and piloting, and piloting itself is an important and very challenging aspect of research. The problem of how researchers find out what they want to know becomes increasingly well-defined with training and experience, but the problem of how researchers find out what their respondents have to say, or know but cannot say, usually remains ill-defined. Ethnographic methods offer more opportunities for addressing this critical issue, but are more time consuming and often difficult to negotiate. They are also limited by the quality of relationships in the field and the ability of the researcher to put aside their ‘taken for granted’ assumptions and pick up, clarify and confirm alternative frames of reference. Good researchers may be able to elicit their clients’ well-defined problems, but find it difficult to infer their ill-defined problems. They may pick up clues about their respondents’ concerns, norms and feelings without understanding how they interact and how, if at all, they are conceptualized. If we now turn to teaching with a focus on learners’ well-defined and ill-defined problems, many different issues emerge. Trainees and qualified practitioners learn in three types of setting: education settings; practice settings; and other spaces at home, college or work. Most of those who support their learning use methods specific to just one type of setting and give most of their attention to learning within that type of setting, even when the object of that learning is elsewhere. Not only does learning take place very differently, but the discourse is also very different in these settings. Initiatives such as problem-based learning, enquiry-based learning and structured distance learning have begun to attack this problem; and some teachers design mediating texts and diagrams to facilitate learning across these boundaries. However, large swathes of professional education still ignore the disconnections between the discourses of education, research and practice. The conceptualizations of theory held by lecturers are usually well defined, but their conceptualizations of practice may be limited by their own recent experience, and the opposite may be true for most practitioners. However, most learners and newly qualified practitioners have not yet reached this stage, and they either find both domains ill-defined or else they use dangerously simplistic constructs and frameworks. Can students be given skeleton concept maps or case notes to carry across settings and adapt and expand, in order both to record and to anticipate their learning progress? Those areas which continue to cause them difficulty should then be prioritized for rethinking across different learning settings. Currently, most teachers see themselves as being only responsible for learning in education settings, practitioners are only responsible for learning in practice settings, and learners are solely responsible for their learning in other settings. Few people are allocated any space or time for focusing on ‘joined up’ learning. While several significant initiatives have been reported in this and other journals, these examples remain the exception rather than the rule. Another important issue is what does or does not happen in practice settings when experienced practitioners encounter ill-defined problems. The main methods are to get more evidence (although it may not be clear what evidence is most relevant in ill-defined cases), to use trial and error, and to obtain new perspectives from the following: consultations with people who have a different range of knowledge and experience; looking at the wider context of the situation; or using the lenses provided by potentially relevant theories. Often several of these methods will be used; and where teams are involved, they may need to change their normal working patterns in order to focus on eliciting and evaluating novel ideas. Disconnections between discourses and frames of reference are also prominent in practice settings. In healthcare, there are still major disconnections between the methods and expertise of different professions and of different specialties and subspecialties within those professions; and these are becoming more serious as the average age of patients rises and their problems increasingly extend across several specialties. Another disconnection, now finally receiving serious attention, is the disconnection between supporting healthy living, on the one hand, and providing curative healthcare, on the other. Healthcare policy is now being recognized as an ill-defined problem, which needs to be tackled at all levels simultaneously; and I would argue that the best place to start, especially in higher education, is at the level of the individual client. For elderly clients, in particular, daily living is often the key to wellbeing. Clients are most likely to follow professional advice when it fits into the routines and spaces of daily living and when its significance for wellbeing is understood, not only by clients but also by friends, relations and carers. What are the client's routines, and can they be used in a positive way? What is a well-defined problem for clients, which they can work out for themselves once they recognize what is needed? What are the ill-defined problems that cause them concern, about which they worry and feel they cannot do anything? Are there good ways of adapting their daily routines? How, with professional help, can they retune them to match their new situation? The achievement of this mutual understanding of a client's needs and appropriate responses depends on the quality of clients’ communications with health professionals and the co-ordination between those health professionals. But how does such communication fit into the concept of ‘method’? Is it best treated as another type of method or as an additional dimension of method? In either case, communication needs more attention from most health and social care workers, and its role in professional development needs to be expanded. It is intended to raise more questions than answers.
