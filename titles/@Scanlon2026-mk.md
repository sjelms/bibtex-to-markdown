> This video short, titled "How to Fix the Housing Crisis" (hosted by economic commentator and Vanderbilt Policy Accelerator Senior Fellow Kyla Scanlon), analyzes systemic solutions to America's national deficit of nearly five million housing units. Challenging the prevailing consensus that local zoning deregulation alone can resolve housing unaffordability, the clip synthesizes two policy publications from the Vanderbilt Policy Accelerator (VPA) to advocate for an "all-of-the-above" federal strategy that combines market-shaping administrative action with direct public intervention. First, the video outlines key takeaways from Expanding Affordable Housing Supply without Legislation: A Plan for Scaling Community-Owned Housing by Dave Uejio. It highlights how a presidential administration can use existing executive authorities to scale community-owned housing—such as Community Land Trusts (CLTs) and resident-owned communities—without waiting for congressional legislation. The short highlights actionable administrative levers, including updating automated underwriting tools at Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, reforming Federal Home Loan Bank grant targeting, and establishing a dedicated Community Ownership Capital Facility to connect community land trusts with secondary market liquidity. Second, drawing on War Housing by Christopher Serkin and Daniel Sharfstein, the video contextualizes modern supply constraints through the historical precedent of World War II. It details how the federal government brought nearly two million wartime housing units online despite severe labor and material shortages by taking on direct roles in housing construction, financing reform, prefabrication research, and adaptive reuse. Scanlon argues that relying solely on private market filtering fails to generate sufficient affordable inventory, asserting that contemporary housing policy must resurrect WWII-era ambition by deploying direct government building, targeted federal credit windows, and innovative property ownership models alongside land-use reforms.
