> [!abstract]
> In recent decades, scholars and policymakers have been focused primarily on deregulatory solutions to America's acute housing crisis, promoting looser land use regulations to allow more private development. This policy myopia ignores an important history of more robust and creative interventions. During World War II, the war effort almost foundered due to a critical shortage of housing for millions of defense workers migrating to new industrial centers. The Roosevelt Administration's innovative responses reveal a much broader range of policies that governments can adopt to promote housing affordability, reforms that truly meet the scale of a housing crisis. The federal government's reforms swept through housing finance and construction. While stimulating private markets by lowering borrowing costs, the government also directly intervened to boost housing supply and served as an incubator for innovation. It modeled conversions of underused buildings into dense housing, piloted new forms of collective ownership, promoted research and development on construction and design, and partnered with leading architects, including Frank Lloyd Wright, to create models of multi-family housing with community commons that could serve as blueprints for new ways of living. The government itself built hundreds of thousands of units, developing new, low-cost construction techniques. While Roosevelt eventually retreated from the most innovative policies in the face of opposition from anti-New Deal and private real estate interests, the U.S. nevertheless succeeded in bringing two million new housing units online during the war years despite daunting shortages of labor and materials. This Article uses the history of the World War II housing crisis as a lens for understanding the poverty of today's policy conversations. Ultimately, the Article reveals how housing crises require a full spectrum of policy responses beyond merely relaxing zoning rules. The Article demonstrates how much more aggressively policymakers could act today if they treated the current housing crisis like the crisis it is.
